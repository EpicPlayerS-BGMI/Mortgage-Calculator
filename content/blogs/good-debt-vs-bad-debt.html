Good Debt vs Bad Debt: Understanding the Difference

⚖️

Debt Quality Depends on Purpose, Price, and Payback Path

“Good debt” usually refers to borrowing that funds an asset or skill likely to increase income or net worth over time—think carefully sized education, a productive business loan, or a affordable home mortgage. “Bad debt” typically funds rapidly depreciating consumption at high interest, especially revolving credit-card balances for lifestyle spending.

These labels are heuristics, not moral laws. A mortgage can become harmful if the payment crushes cash flow. A small appliance loan can be tolerable if the rate is low and payoff is scheduled. The useful question is whether the debt’s expected benefit exceeds its cost with margin for error.

This article gives decision tests, examples across India/USA/UK contexts, and calculator links to quantify EMIs before you sign.

A Simple Three-Part Test

Purpose: Does the loan buy future earning power or long-lived utility, or today’s fleeting want? Price: Is the interest rate far above expected investment returns or wage growth? Path: Do you have a written payoff schedule that still leaves room for emergency savings?

If purpose is weak, price is high, and path is vague, classify it as debt to avoid or eliminate first. If purpose is strong, price is competitive, and path is clear, it may be manageable leverage.

Add a fourth filter when the first three look acceptable: stress. Can you still make the payment if income drops 20% or the floating rate rises 2%? Debt that only works in a perfect month is leverage without a margin of safety—and margins of safety are what keep “good” debt from flipping labels.

Examples That Challenge Slogans

A student loan for a credential with strong employment odds can be good debt; the same loan for an unfocused program with poor placement data can be risky. A car loan for a reliable commute vehicle at a fair rate may be justified; financing a luxury car at 84 months when income is unstable leans bad.

In Mumbai, a home loan on a starter flat with a payment under prudent FOIR limits may build long-term housing stability. In the US, borrowing on a credit card for a wedding upgrade at 24% APR is classic bad debt—even if the day was memorable. In the UK, an interest-free promotional purchase can be fine only if you can repay before the promo ends.

Regional Nuances

India: distinguish productive MSME credit from consumption borrowing on cards. Watch processing fees and insurance add-ons that raise effective cost. USA: mortgage interest and student loans have distinct underwriting and (sometimes) tax treatments—verify current rules. UK: beware expensive overdraft and catalogue credit marketed as convenience.

Using Leverage Without Letting It Use You

Cap total EMI burden, keep an emergency fund, and refuse debt that only works if everything goes perfectly. Revisit labels annually: yesterday’s reasonable auto loan can become today’s burden after a pay cut.

  • Compare interest cost to the benefit you can measure.
  • Prefer EMI plans you can stress-test at a higher rate.
  • Eliminate high-APR revolving balances before adding new “good” debt.
  • Document why you borrowed within one written paragraph.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

People mislabel debt when slogans replace cash-flow math. These are the classification errors that show up most often:

  • Calling every mortgage automatically good regardless of size.
  • Assuming 0% financing is free when deferred interest traps apply.
  • Taking education debt without researching outcomes.
  • Using home equity to fund vacations and relabelling it “investment.”
  • Ignoring currency or income shocks when leveraged heavily.

Case Studies: Relabeling Debt After the Fact

Case one: a home loan taken at a prudent payment ratio remains productive even when prices soften temporarily, because the household can still pay and stay. Case two: the same sized loan on a speculative second property with short rental history becomes fragile—purpose looked like investment, but cash-flow buffers were missing. Labels follow resilience, not marketing language.

Case three: an education loan for a programme with strong placement data and a clear income step-up plan is easier to defend than borrowing for a vague credential with poor outcomes. Case four: a 0% retail plan that is not paid before the promo ends can morph into expensive deferred interest—bad debt wearing a friendly mask.

Create a one-paragraph debt thesis for every new loan: purpose, rate, payoff date, and what sacrifice funds the payments. If you cannot write the paragraph, do not sign. Review old theses yearly; a car loan that made sense at a stable job may deserve acceleration after a raise, while a low-rate mortgage might not deserve obsessive prepayment if retirement accounts are empty.

Teach teenagers and new graduates the framework early. Distinguishing leverage from lifestyle credit prevents years of avalanche maths later. Good debt is a tool with a manual; bad debt is a subscription to regret.

Interest Cost Versus Asset Outcome Scorecard

A useful habit is scoring each debt on two axes: annualised interest cost and expected asset/income outcome. High cost + weak outcome is the payoff-first pile. Low cost + strong outcome can stay if payments fit a stress-tested budget.

Credit card revolving 18–36% Bad Paid in full monthly
Primary home loan Lower secured rates Often good Payment crushes FOIR/DTI

Lifestyle personal loan High Bad Rarely flips

Revisit the scorecard after job changes or rate resets. Yesterday’s productive mortgage can become fragile; a short personal loan that clears a 30% card can temporarily be the least-bad option.

EMI Cost Face-Off: Mortgage, Card Balance, and Education Loan

Labels get clearer when you price identical principal at different rates. Take ₹5 lakh (or roughly $6,000) borrowed three ways—illustrative only—and compare five-year cost. A home-loan top-up slice at 8.5% EMI is about ₹10,250 per month; total interest over 60 months lands near ₹1.15 lakh. The same principal as a revolving card balance at 36% APR with a “comfortable” minimum-style repayment can cost several times more interest if you only chip away slowly; even a disciplined five-year amortising payoff at 24% still costs roughly ₹3.4 lakh in interest—triple the mortgage slice.

An education loan at 10.5% on ₹5 lakh for five years sits in the middle: EMI near ₹10,750 and total interest around ₹1.45 lakh. That mid-priced debt is “good” only if the credential raises after-tax income enough to cover payments with buffer. Without placement odds, it is simply expensive leverage for a certificate.

Borrowing path Illustrative rate 60-mo EMI (₹5L) Approx. total interest
Home-loan style 8.5% ≈₹10,250 ≈₹1.15 lakh
Education loan 10.5% ≈₹10,750 ≈₹1.45 lakh
Card-like APR (amortised) 24% ≈₹14,400 ≈₹3.65 lakh

In dollars: $6,000 at 7% for 60 months costs about $1,130 interest; at 22% the interest balloons past $3,900. Run your own figures on the EMI Calculator, Personal Loan Calculator, and Mortgage Calculator before you inherit a slogan.

FOIR and DTI Stress Test Before You Call Debt “Good”

Indian lenders often watch FOIR (fixed obligation to income ratio); US underwriting watches DTI (debt-to-income). A household with ₹1,20,000 monthly take-home and ₹42,000 existing EMIs already sits at 35% FOIR. Adding a ₹28,000 “affordable” car EMI pushes obligations to ₹70,000—about 58%—leaving little room for rent/society charges, groceries, or a job hiccup. On paper the car loan funds a commute asset; in cash-flow terms it can be bad debt wearing a productive mask.

US parallel: $7,200 gross monthly income with $2,100 existing debts is ~29% DTI. A new $1,900 mortgage payment lifts DTI near 56% before taxes and childcare—fragile even if the house is “an investment.” Stress the stack at +2% rate and −20% income. If either scenario forces credit-card float to survive, pause the new loan.

Write the test in one line: “At stressed income, total EMIs stay under X% and emergency fund stays ≥3 months.” If you cannot fill X with a number your sleep accepts, the debt is not yet good enough to take.

Scorecard with Hard Numbers: Rate, Payback, and Buffer

Expand the qualitative scorecard into three scored fields out of ten. Rate score: 10 if APR is near secured-loan levels, 2 if it is card-like. Payback score: 10 if a calendar date clears the balance within a horizon you control, 3 if “someday.” Buffer score: 10 if FOIR/DTI stays under your stress ceiling with cash reserves intact, 1 if the payment needs overtime forever.

Example: Priya’s ₹40 lakh home loan at 8.7% with EMI at 32% of take-home and six months of expenses in FDs might score Rate 8, Payback 7, Buffer 8—keep and review annually. Marcus’s $4,500 revolving card balance at 26% with only minimum payments scores Rate 2, Payback 2, Buffer 3—avalanche first. Aisha’s £12,000 postgraduate loan at 6% with a clear salary step-up offer scores Rate 7, Payback 6, Buffer 7—conditional keep, with a written refinance or extra-payment plan after the first promotion.

Any debt scoring under 15 combined points becomes a payoff priority regardless of marketing language. Re-score after raises, rate resets, and family changes; numbers drift even when slogans stay sticky.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is credit-card debt always bad?

Revolving balances at high APR for consumption usually are. Paying in full monthly can make a card a convenient tool rather than bad debt.

Can a personal loan be good debt?

Sometimes—if it consolidates costlier debt or funds a clear income-producing need at a lower rate, with a fixed payoff plan.

Are business loans good debt?

They can be, when expected cash flows cover repayments with a buffer. Many small-business loans fail that test—underwrite yourself strictly.

Should I avoid all debt?

Not necessarily. Some goals are harder without credit. Avoiding high-cost consumer debt while using selective leverage is a common balanced approach.

How do I prioritise payoff?

Attack the worst-priced debt first if you can stay motivated, or use snowball wins—then keep a rule against replacing them.
